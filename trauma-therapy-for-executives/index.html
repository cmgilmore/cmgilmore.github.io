For executives and leaders

Therapy for leaders who look fine on paper

Trauma, burnout, and high-functioning anxiety in people who are very good at holding it all together. What it looks like, why it happens, and how therapy helps.

High-functioning trauma is when past or ongoing stress keeps your nervous system on guard while you keep performing at a high level. It often shows up in leaders as constant vigilance, perfectionism, trouble resting, and strong reactions to criticism. Trauma-focused therapy such as EMDR can reduce those reactions so drive comes from choice rather than threat.

Why so many leaders carry more than they show

Leadership rewards a specific set of skills: scanning for problems before they happen, staying calm while others panic, putting your own needs last, and never dropping the ball. For many people, those skills weren’t learned in an MBA program. They were learned much earlier, in a home that was unpredictable, a family where love felt conditional on achievement, or a stretch of life where being competent was the only thing that felt safe.

Those adaptations work. They often work so well that they become a career. The cost tends to show up later, when the stakes are higher, the pace never lets up, and the body starts to keep score in the form of insomnia, irritability, tension, or a flatness that’s hard to name.

The traits that make you effective and the patterns that are wearing you down are often the same traits, running without an off switch.

Signs of high-functioning trauma and anxiety

You don’t need a dramatic history for these to apply. Common patterns I see in leaders include:

  • Hypervigilance. Constantly reading the room, anticipating problems, and feeling responsible for everyone’s reactions.
  • Inability to rest. Downtime brings guilt or restlessness rather than relief. Weekends are for catching up.
  • Outsized reactions to feedback. Knowing a critique is minor but feeling it as a threat for days.
  • Perfectionism and over-preparation. Working three times harder than needed to avoid any chance of failing.
  • Emotional numbness. Big wins feel strangely empty. Relationships feel like another thing to manage.
  • Body signals you’ve learned to ignore. Jaw clenching, shallow breathing, stomach issues, waking at 3 a.m. with your mind already running.
  • Old memories that still have a charge. An event from years ago that still tightens your chest when it comes up.

Burnout or trauma? Often both

Burnout is usually described as exhaustion, cynicism, and a sense of reduced effectiveness from chronic workplace stress. Rest and boundaries help with burnout, but for many leaders they don’t stick. That’s often because something underneath makes rest feel unsafe, or makes saying no feel like a risk to belonging. When we work on that underlying layer, the practical changes (delegating, logging off, taking real time away) tend to become much easier to sustain.

How therapy helps

1. Stabilize first

We start by building practical tools to notice and shift your nervous system state in real time: before a hard conversation, after a tense meeting, or at 3 a.m. This is grounded in polyvagal theory and is useful from the first few sessions.

2. Understand the pattern

We map where your patterns came from and what they’re protecting. Most leaders find relief in seeing their reactions as adaptations that made sense, rather than as personal failures.

3. Process what’s stuck

Using EMDR, we work directly on the memories and beliefs (“I’m only valuable when I’m producing,” “If I relax, something bad will happen”) that keep your system on alert. The goal is for those memories to feel like the past instead of the present.

4. Lead from a steadier place

As the threat response quiets, people often notice they still care deeply about their work, but from choice rather than fear. Decisions get clearer, relationships get warmer, and rest starts to feel like rest.

What working with me is like

I spent nearly two decades in executive leadership in higher education before becoming a therapist. I understand board pressure, budget seasons, managing up and down at the same time, and the loneliness of being the person everyone else leans on. That background helps us skip a lot of explaining and get to the real work.

Sessions are direct and collaborative. I’ll be honest with you, I’ll respect your time, and I won’t ask you to perform. Many of my clients say therapy is the one hour of the week where they don’t have to be impressive.
